# Orientation

Political meaning in the United States, founded during the intellectual backdrop of the Age of Enlightenment, has been inseparable from values of reason and liberty. But has a direction informed by Enlightenment philosophy reached its breaking point? Economic and social inequity highlight the shortcomings, e.g.,
- freedom of opportunity -> [extreme concentration of wealth](https://realtimeinequality.org/)
- self-evident truths -> [disagreement on basic facts](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/07/30/most-americans-say-republican-and-democratic-voters-cannot-agree-on-basic-facts/)
- personal property -> [subscriptions and licensing](https://acr-journal.com/article/subscription-economy-and-the-transformation-of-ownership-a-review-of-value-perceptions-and-retention-strategies-1772/)
- resisting government -> [youth precarity/disillusionment](https://ppp-online.org/view-all-volumes/editorial-ppp-special-issue-contemporary-youth-precarity-resistance-and-reimagined-futures/)
- or maybe, like the Renaissance before it, ideas of progress were absorbed by the realities of social order before they were actualized. ==not 100% sure if this is how I want to structure an intro, I might do more to contrast familiar models of social justice==

How do we address issues of inequity? While taboo in mainstream American politics, **Marxist** theory offers insight to how economic structures exacerbate domination... unfortunately, even that isn't enough to understand our time:
- Class interests are not always the primary driver in a diverse society ([stratification economics](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/William-Darity/publication/226437749_Stratification_economics_The_role_of_intergroup_inequality/links/00b7d51f25d3a3fa36000000/Stratification-economics-The-role-of-intergroup-inequality.pdf)), and technology has evolved in some ways that make models of industrial capitalism obsolete ([technofeudalism](https://books.google.com/books?hl=en&lr=&id=9k2lEAAAQBAJ&oi=fnd&pg=PT7&dq=technofeudalism&ots=bz6J6xnoUI&sig=UO2PJI6ZuTwak-ipN5n4z8hv47c)).
- It is also unclear if even 'overthrowing the powerful' would be sufficient, as we might extrapolate from **Du Bois**... problems will manifest elsewhere without also addressing the complexities that bring problems forward ([the philadelphia negro](https://books.google.com/books?hl=en&lr=&id=qqkJAAAAIAAJ&oi=fnd&pg=PR3&dq=philadelphia+negro&ots=Buvt18pGBj&sig=kF2mmKRrhHJP6gnR8nQTC8dZTOk#v=onepage&q=philadelphia%20negro&f=false)).

So what incentivizes domination in our society, why is it reproduced in the first place? Power situated in culture and institutions cannot be understood in isolation. To truly understand inequity, we must consider the interconnectedness of everything. 
- For this reason, we turn to **Western Marxists** that eschewed a reductive orthodox focus on economics (**Gramsci**, **Lukács**) and instead questioned the presumed rationality of institutional and cultural artifacts (**Adorno**, **Marcuse**). This tradition informs us of social processes that maintain the existing order and prohibit social change.
- Other post-Marxist schools of thought that share this totalistic view of social relations offer insight to what we can do to *address* processes of hegemonic domination. **Stuart Hall** and his work in **Cultural Studies** demonstrate how to identify dominant culture and its resistance. **Laclau and Mouffe** suggest hegemony is a necessary reflection of society and must be the target of change.

To return to an understanding of inequity grounded in totality, we must identify 1) the processes of social conflict that constructed society, 2) how individual and group subjectivity forms, and 3) the resulting collective forces that prevent change to the status quo. Those three goals are reflected in the blueprint of [[Pleromism\|Pleromism]].
- First, I will describe the intellectual lineage for the components of Pleromism.
- Then, I will detail research methods to explore this blueprint to analyze policy and inform opportunities for change.
- Finally, I will introduce cyberspace as a social development that brings both extreme potential and threat that Pleromism uniquely helps us understand.

# 1. Power

[[10 - 19 Power/10. Power\|Power]] is the force that shapes social relations and manifests in many ways. It is responsible for the network of boundaries that determine what we can or cannot do, but what is the *form* of power in its fullest sense? This section will build out a typology of power.

### Active / [[10 - 19 Power/12. Negotiators\|Negotiators]]

Power used by actors for domination was developed as the 'three dimensions of power.' This tradition begins with a formula established by **Robert Dahl**, where A gets B to do something they wouldn't otherwise do.
- **Bachrach & Baratz** contended this formula only describes visible acts of power; domination can also be *hidden* when it occurs by excluding voices from agenda-setting.
- **Steven Lukes** further added the *invisible* dimension of power, activated when those who are advantaged exert ideological influence so the oppressed support their own oppression (note: aligns w/ **Bourdieu**'s concept of symbolic violence).

The three dimensions of power are helpful to represent the various active processes of power to constrict what is possible for that who is on the receiving end. However, this alone is insufficient. Critiques include:
- Power conceptualized as a quantifiable amount that is 'held' implies an equitable society comes from redistributing that power. That lacks consideration of the role non-material concepts (e.g., culture, agency) have in oppression (**Iris Marion Young**).
- Analytical bias on how 'the powerless' are oppressed fails to consider how 'the powerful' are also influenced by social boundaries (**Clarissa Hayward**).

Because of these limits, we will now consider a parallel tradition of thought.

### Passive / [[10 - 19 Power/13. Emitters\|Emitters]]

The work of **Michel Foucault** makes the case that power can be productive just as it is oppressive. It is diffuse and shapes who people are and what they choose to do. Knowledge creates individual subjectivity and as a result the social order, which is materialized in techniques, organizations, roles, etc.
- Similar logic was preempted by the ethnographies of subcultures conducted by **W. E. B. Du Bois** and then members of the early Chicago School sociologists (**Thrasher**, **Frazier**), who concluded that subcultures could only be understood by relation to the context in which they exist (i.e., institutions and culture).
- Further, **Pierre Bourdieu** described habitus, or the learned dispositions that incline agents to act/react in certain ways and were developed as a result of social relations despite now being seen as 'normal.'

The throughline among these thinkers is that people are shaped not just by active domination, but we constantly participate in a world that shapes who we are whether we realize it or not. This contradicts the idea that power is inherently negative and is instead contingent on how it is used.
- This occurs through government programs, cultural expressions like language, and so on. Because those components were themselves constructed, we can understand the continued effects as a reproduction of the negotiation that shaped them. 

Regardless, this characterization of power has also been met with critique:
- For the purposes of social equity, it becomes impossible to differentiate between "good" and "bad" uses of power if everyone is produced by it. This can strip oppressors of accountability and leave an unclear path forward.
- If power is everywhere, then it is nowhere. But if it is not 'held' by anyone, what can explain why there are clearly differentials in who is able to exert mechanisms of power with corresponding differences in scale and scope?

Such questions may be reconciled with one more concept.

### Potential / [[10 - 19 Power/11. Capacitors\|Capacitors]]

**Bourdieu** extends the concept of capital beyond merely that of economic means. Cultural, symbolic, and social capital can all enable one's ability to shape social relations (i.e., use mechanisms of power). We can understand such capital to not necessarily be power itself; rather, money, relationships, credentials, and so on are accumulative potential for power.
- Depending on one's social position in a collective, they may have more or less access to certain types of capital. The properties of each type influences how power manifests, although they can be exchanged under certain conditions dictated by a field (*described in Section 2*).

### Vessels / [[10 - 19 Power/14. Conduits\|Conduits]]

Finally, we must who or what is responsible for mechanisms of power if people do not 'hold' power. Applying the tradition of **social constructionism** to the types of power that have been discussed, we understand that components like institutions and cultures are simultaneously outlets for and targets of power. *Individuals* are outlets for and targets of power.
- This is perhaps best understood through the social construction of artifacts, or symmetry, from **Pinch & Bijker**. They describe how technology is the product of social forces and mixed motives, and artifacts have overlapping functions but ultimately serve some social interests better.
- **Bruno Latour**'s Actor-Network Theory builds upon this idea by de-emphasizing human intention and including the naturally causal effects of things. There is co-production of human actors and even nonhuman objects in the world in which they are found.

These ideas lead us back to reinforce the conceptualization of power described by **Clarissa Hayward** at the beginning of this section: power is the network of social boundaries that shape possible action.
- The (innumerable?) artifacts that inform us of what is or not possible - religion, government, family, language, etc. - are the nodes in this network of boundaries. All of them have been shaped by power and shape us with power.
- In the context of equity, this means boundaries can be expanded or contracted. The question for social justice should be to what extent people have the agency to shape their own boundaries.

# 2. Collectives

We just discussed power's effects resulting in a network that defines what is or is not possible: this network constitutes a [[30 - 39 Realms/30. Realms\|Realm]] of Freedom. It includes what is normal, good, legal, rational, and other qualifiers that, if violated, would result in the individual facing consequences. 

### [[30 - 39 Realms/30. Realms\|Realms]]

While the previous section on power is applicable to the context of both individuals and collectives, there are additional factors to consider for collectives. Because individual Realms can significantly differ among those who are part of a group (i.e., a different configuration of their Conduits), a new consciousness in the form of a [[30 - 39 Realms/32. Collective Realms\|Collective Realm]] emerges.
- From **Émile Durkheim**, we understand there to be collective representations that help regulate behavior and relationships among individuals, both which allow integration to common structures and needs of society. These standards are formal or informal. For example, the law acts as a collective representation of that which should be legally binding. 
- Further affirming the importance of Conduits, Durkheim stresses the need to analyze beyond the flow of social relations and also consider institutional structures and processes to understand society.

**Max Weber** again highlights the interrelatedness of culture and institutions in his analysis of Protestant values on the economic system of Capitalism. Of course, not every individual subject to this economic system is Protestant, but all must participate in it.
- Given that the individual Realms in a Collective Realm do not perfectly overlap, some components will necessarily be excluded. For example, if society sets a standard for what is illegal, a violation will brand someone a criminal and their Realm shrinks as a result of imprisonment.
- Such conflict can appear in less 'obvious' ways as a reflection of dominant values, which is typified by anti-Black racism. Government policies have explicitly excluded Black people through discrimination, their exploitation was culturally normalized (**Charles W. Mills**), and even the methodological basis of scientific rationality has unfairly shaped our notion of truth (**Zuberi & Bonilla-Silva**).

### [[20 - 29 Myths/20. Myths\|Myths]]

We have some explanation, from Power Capacitors, that certain individuals or groups have a greater ability to shape the boundaries of themselves and others. That also includes influence on the Collective Realm. But what compels groups to dominate other groups in a collective instead of percieving them as equals?

Here we must consider the contributions, awkward as they may be, of Structuralism. **Ferdinand de Saussure** identified how common structures or patterns of thought recurred throughout societies, and **Claude Lévi-Strauss** applied these ideas to study how universal stories and myths can be broken down into fundamental relations between a function and a subject based on the originating culture.
- In some ways, the structural determinativeness is strongly contradictory to the agency present in Pleromic thought. Structuralism was also extremely influenced by **Cybernetics**, a field that had great impact on the development of cyberspace and emergent issues (further explored in Section 5).
- Yet simultaneously, Structuralism resulted in critique from **Phenomenologists** (universal features of how consciousness is experienced) and **Post-Structuralists** (who agree structures exist but are constructed through power) that have largely informed Pleromism.
- Uhhh should probably mention **Louis Althusser** and Structural Marxism here, which informed Cultural Studies. His description of ideology, as a symbolic representation of real conditions, is related here.

The point is, myths helps groups rationalize the world around them. They take historical meaning and present it as natural (**Roland Barthes**). Because collectives are composed of contradictory elements, myths are symbolic resolutions of these contradictions (**Frederic Jameson**) and can suture the dislocated social order (**Laclau and Mouffe**). 

### [[20 - 29 Myths/25. Mythoscapes\|Mythoscapes]]

Myths do not just exist in isolation, though. **Bourdieu** describes the concept of fields, or structured social arenas that have their own rules. More than two people getting together, but less than an entire culture.
- His conception includes contexts like art, politics, and literature, although Pleromism extends these structures to include arenas of identity: race, class, and so on. Each has symbiotic relationships with myths of their making (or assigning). 
- Individuals are not confined to one Mythoscape, nor do they always have stagnant or permanent memberships. There is a complex relationship with overlapping identities where their co-existence also creates something entirely new, as described by Critical Race Theory scholarship on intersectionality (**Crenshaw**, **Hill Collins**).

### [[30 - 39 Realms/32. Collective Realms\|Collective Realms]]

Now that components of Realms are understood as infinite sets of circles within circles... we can now identify *what* the dominant Realm in a collective is, or rather what it excludes. **J. Milton Yinger**'s work in methodologically defining countercultures show how groups that define their system of norms in explicit opposition to the mainstream illuminate what is 'not acceptable.'
- Dominant culture can be understood through the dialectic, where the counterculture existing outside of it shows that there is an "it."
- (side note: Yinger's concept is semi-related to, but in this case must be separated from, the colloquial usage associated with the counterculture of the 1960s. Further explained in Section 3.)

This logic is paralleled and expanded on by **Stuart Hall**, who more explicitly engaged with Marxism and rejected the mainstream Parsonian sociological tradition in the United States. His work in Cultural Studies demonstrated how even subcultures exposes and resist dominant culture by distinguishing themselves.
- Probably worth engaging with the "post-subcultural" critics of Hall here, since they reinforce his argument imo by unquestioningly accepting pluralism while ignoring power relations.

# 3. Stability

The final piece to social relations are the processes by which a Collective Realm retains its shape and resists significant change. Even without a particular 'ruling class,' even without intent, the status quo largely enforces existing relationships between groups in society through six processes. These six processes are distinct but at times have overlapping functions.

Note that as a stylistic choice to avoid some harms of over-rationalized science, this research refers to the social processes listed below as though they are mythological cosmic deities - the [[40 - 49 Realm Enforcers/40. Realm Enforcers\|Realm Enforcers]] :)

First, [[40 - 49 Realm Enforcers/41. Monadus\|Monadus]] represents hegemony, as described by **Antonio Gramsci** and expanded on by **Stuart Hall**. This is the process by which a certain representation of a collective takes a leading, defining position. This leadership is not necessarily domination; it has the capacity to contain, educate, and reshape oppositional forces without removing them entirely. It manifests as the common-sense assumptions and the taken-for-granted.

Second, [[40 - 49 Realm Enforcers/42. Mithrot\|Mithrot]] represents the process by which we imbue meaning in myths. Myths are detotalizing in nature, since they represent one perspective. By representing patterns, they also tend to oversimplify the situations they are symbols of. Thirdly, their form can be copied even if substance is changed. Some examples of the latter appropriation or "*mythdirection*" include:
- Misrecognition from **Bourdieu**, where cultural or social capital is converted in a way that conceals its origin and makes the appropriator seem authentic.
- Metanarratives from **Jean-François Lyotard**, where supposedly universal ideas (myths) are legitimized by the anticipation of that yet-unrealized ideal.
- Hyperreality from **Jean Baudrillard**, where fearful citizens are seduced into a simulated version of reality when society seems unstable.

Third, [[40 - 49 Realm Enforcers/43. Categorius\|Categorius]] represents the process by which we imbue meaning in classified knowledge. As described by **Foucault**, the subjectification of people produces who they become, at which point governments exert biopower to manage those populations. It also represents the dividing practices or binaries leveraged in myth.

Fourth, [[40 - 49 Realm Enforcers/44. Rationaliza\|Rationaliza]] represents the process by which the status quo is normalized, justified, or otherwise made out to be the superior or only option. This was partially discussed with earlier reference to symmetry, but also includes:
- Reification from **György Lukács**, where our social constructions become living things or objective facts we are then effected by. **Theodor Adorno** and **Max Horkheimer** link the concept to Reason and the drive for everything to become something measured or used.
- Governmentality from **Foucault**, where expert technical decisions are depoliticized and made out to seem objective and/or inevitable.
- Technologic rationality from **Herbert Marcuse** (and later echoed by those 1960s counterculture writers like **Charles Reich** and **Theodore Roszak** on the technocracy), where it becomes impossible to question the 'experts' and their 'science' towards 'progress'.

Fifth, [[40 - 49 Realm Enforcers/45. Distributon\|Distribution]] represents the process by which resources are allocated among members in a collective. Because our society's economic system is structured such that wealth or debt accumulates, Distributon is currently split into two forms: Hoarde and Deprivas.
- These effects are the most visible among the Enforcers and can be explored either through orthodox Marxism or contemporary researchers on inequality.

Sixth, [[40 - 49 Realm Enforcers/46. Repressio\|Repressio]] represents the process by which unacceptable terms in a collective are limited. This can occur through visible force (e.g., imprisonment, silencing), or in more subtle forms. The later could look like repressive desublimation from **Herbert Marcuse**, where desires and oppositions are allowed but defanged, or implicit censorship from **Judith Butler**, where the unspeakable is ruled out before it is spoken.

It is possible that the listed Enforcers have more functions than described. It is also possible there are more than six Enforcers. This is just an early attempt at weaving together similarly-minded concepts that appear to be describing related functions.
- These were also settled upon with influence from the field of Western Esotericism. I refer not to esoteric traditions themselves (although some traces of Neoplatonism, Gnosticism, Hermeticism, etc. can be found here and there), but instead to the study of these traditions and their relation to orthodox from the likes of **Wouter Hanegraaff**, **Elane Pagels**, and others. Certain processes of 'defining the mainstream' have been occurring since the first century or earlier.

Anyway. So the whole point of all of this is... if people want a *different kind of society* - a more equitable kind, hopefully - these six processes are what they would need to contend with. Knowing is half the battle, I suppose.

# 4. Methods

Pleromism defines standardized categories to observe social relations, conflict, and stability in collectives. This blueprint can then serve as reference for [[Pleromic Analysis\|Pleromic Analysis]].
- This section is not yet built out, but the intention is to grasp totality in a given situation to the extent that the researcher can identify the appropriate targets for effective social change
- This is not paired with direct, systematic instruction; instead, it is more like a set of tools to be applied (incompletely) at the researcher's discretion based on theory

An effective analysis hinges on three questions: what has shaped what we know, and how is meaning actively being shaped, and how can change occur?
- Each component of Pleromism is sort of like a box on a checklist... though there are different paths to understand each. Conduits require genealogy, for example, to uncover how they developed and what interests shaped their current form.
- Methods developed in Discourse Analysis are highly relevant, such as negotiators being further informed by **J. L. Austin**'s work on Speech Act Theory, differentiating locution (what was said), illocution (what was intended), and perlocution (the actual effect). Indexicality, genre, etc.

There are also two additional categories that should be consulted for Pleromic Analysis (though they haven't really been built out yet, either):
- **Realm Expanders** are processes conducive to illuminating other Mythoscapes, increasing notions of freedom, and so on. Think Art, Community, Education, and so on.
- **Power Generators** are the groups of people that have historically been outside the dominant Realm and have found ways to resist. What can be learned from them and applied to the scenario at hand?

# 5. Cyberspace

Okay so deeefinitely not done here at all, but the idea is that a whole lot culminates with cyberspace because all of a sudden, there is a new and (in some ways) unrelated Realm. Institutions defined by physical properties do not translate, and their replacement is designed basically just by for-profit companies. These institutions foster norms that jointly shape cyberspace's dominant Realm, which in turn results in Cyber Enforcers (unique manifestations of the same deities) and a translation back to the 'realspace' to the benefit of their creators.

The good news is that this is neither the beginning nor must it need to be the end of the story. 
- Early cyberspace was marked by insane dichotomies of centralized, unfeeling military control (the closed-world discourse described by **Paul Edwards**) and decentralized, interdisciplinary, and experimental collaboration.
- **Stewart Brand** merged the two together, based on his guiding interest in Cybernetics and his heavy influence on the 1960s counterculture. With figures like **John Perry Barlow**, Brand's networks defined the libertarian culture of early cyberspace (as described by **Fred Turner**).

A number of influences to Pleromism critique Cybernetics for its lack of considering meaning - **Marcuse**, **Donna Hayward**, **Jacques Derrida**, and **Gilles Deleuze** to name a few - and this omission seems ironic considering the fate of cyberculture as it was appropriated and extorted by private industry to become what it is now. Perhaps this can provide insight into how we de-normalize negative changes to society that have resulted from modern technologies?
